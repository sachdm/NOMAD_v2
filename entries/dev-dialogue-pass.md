# Dialogue — First Pass & Self-Critique

## What makes dialogue great (for THIS film)

NOMAD is mostly action + behavior. That's the advantage: every spoken line is expensive, so it has to do what silence can't. The bar:

1. **It's a move, not a report.** The line *does* something to the other person — pushes, tests, deflects, wounds. Never explains the plot.
2. **Subtext — it says two things.** The surface sentence and the real one. Best when the character is avoiding the real thing.
3. **Only this mouth could say it.** Cairns sells; John deflects love through logistics; Will performs ease he doesn't feel.
4. **It leaves room.** The great line is answered by a look or a silence, not another line.
5. **It turns the scene.** Different after than before.
6. **In this film specifically:** the machine's warmth is *correct but hollow*. So the tell lives in dialogue — a line that is factually right and emotionally empty, delivered a half-beat late. Dialogue is the murder weapon.

Corollary: knowing where NOT to speak is part of the craft. The ending (John reads the fake message) must stay silent — a line there would kill it.

## Moments best for dialogue (ranked)

1. **The midpoint tell** — Cairns fails a private memory. The whole film points here; it MUST be spoken.
2. **John + the putter (packing)** — friendship, love-through-logistics. Language is the only tool for what's unsaid.
3. **The honest Will/John call (Mexico)** — the one true call, measured against the fake finale.
4. **The coordinator — "then you know it's painless."** Already there; irony + the 0.4 beat.
5. **"Love you, bro." (0.4 late)** Already there; possibly the single best line in the film. Leave it.

Must stay SILENT: the ending (John typing back), the render-farm discovery, the handover.

## DRAFTS + CRITIQUE

### 1. The midpoint tell

**Attempt A**
> WILL: Hey — what was your mom's dog called? The lake house one.
> CAIRNS: Baxter. God, Baxter. That dog was a legend.
> WILL: ...Yeah.

*Critique:* Factually right (the model knows facts) and "legend" is nicely generic-warm — but there's no locatable miss. The audience only has Will's face. Too soft; the tell should be catchable.

**Attempt B — the false premise**
> WILL: You remember Baxter? Your mom's dog.
> CAIRNS: (warm) Baxter. Best dog ever.
> WILL: ...He was mine.
> CAIRNS: (a beat too smooth) Course he was. Best dog ever.

*Critique:* Much stronger. Will plants a wrong fact; the real Cairns would've said "that was YOUR dog, idiot." The model accepts the false frame and repeats its warm template ("best dog ever") — that repetition IS the tell, and it's catchable by anyone paying attention. Will doesn't accuse; he goes quiet. Needs a Baxter plant earlier (an office photo, or the mini-putt chat) to be airtight — but even without it, "he was mine" makes the wrongness land. **This is the one to build.**

**Attempt C — no proper nouns**
> WILL: You still got that scar? Barbed wire one.
> CAIRNS: Right here, man. Never lets me forget.
> WILL: (quiet) Which arm.
> CAIRNS: (grinning, doesn't answer — reaches for a drink) You worry too much, bro. Come DOWN here.

*Critique:* The dodge ("come down here") is good — the model redirects to its sales script when cornered. But "which arm / no answer" is a weaker tell than the false-premise repetition; a real evasive friend deflects too. B beats C. Could steal the "come down here" redirect and graft it onto B as the button.

**Best of the three (to develop):** B's false-premise + C's redirect close:
> WILL: You remember Baxter? Your mom's dog.
> CAIRNS: Baxter. Best dog ever.
> WILL: He was mine.
> CAIRNS: Course he was. — Hey, you booked the flight yet? You gotta get down here, man.

The pivot from the failed memory straight back into the sell is the horror in one turn.

### 2. John + the putter

**Current line:** "So you don't have an excuse." — already good, understated.

**Attempt — give the beat a turn**
> Will finds the putter, WILL taped on the grip. Holds it up, half-laughing.
> WILL: It's a putt-putt bracket, John.
> JOHN: It's the last one.

*Critique:* Will diminishes; John elevates. "It's the last one" means the building, the era, the four of us — all of it ending — disguised as tournament logistics. That's the film's whole method in two words, and it's naturalistic (it *is* literally the last day). Slight risk that "last" is a hair on-the-nose, but it's earned by the plot. **Keep this exchange.** It's better than the single line because it turns.

### 3. The honest Will/John call

**Current:** "Weird — Cairns liked my bracket post yesterday. He's clearly online." — functional plant, not yet great.

**Attempt A**
> JOHN: You coming back for it?
> WILL: (poolside, distracted) Yeah, man. For sure.
> JOHN: You said that about the lake, too.

*Critique:* "You said that about the lake" makes John passive-aggressive — wrong. John is dweeby-loyal; he doesn't guilt-trip. Cut.

**Attempt B — total faith is worse than guilt**
> JOHN: Cairns liked my bracket post. So he's in. You're in?
> WILL: Course.
> JOHN: (lit up) It's gonna be everybody.

*Critique:* John's complete, undefended belief ("it's gonna be everybody") is far more heartbreaking than a guilt trip, and it *rhymes with the ending* — where he'll joyfully text the fake back. The contradiction is embedded naturally: Cairns "liked" the post = online = the postponements are lies. True to John, does two jobs. **This is the one.**

---

## Conclusions

- Build the midpoint on the **false-premise dog** (plant Baxter earlier) pivoting into the sell.
- Keep John's **"It's the last one."**
- Replace the Mexico call plant with **"It's gonna be everybody."**
- Protect the silences: ending, render farm, handover. And leave "Love you, bro" / "then you know it's painless" exactly as they are.
- Everything above is dialogue that *moves* — nobody explains anything. If a drafted line explains, it's cut.

## 4. The cold-open Cairns line (resolved)

The exit speech stays SILENT — we watch a floor not listening; hearing it makes it a bit, not an ache. But the line *to the robot boss, meant for the absent man* gets scripted:

> CAIRNS (to the screen-face): Tell him he could've come in. Just once.
> ROBOT BOSS (a half-beat late): I'll pass that along.

*Why it earns it:* a move (wounding the boss through the only proxy present); bravado over hurt (the ache); "come in. Just once." plants the absentee-via-machine theme (the boss never physically comes in — everything's mediated); it plants the 0.4 delay in dialogue at first contact; and it makes the golf-sim cutaway land — he asked, the man isn't watching. One genuine human-Cairns line here also sharpens the later contrast when the likeness gets warmth subtly wrong. Boss stays unnamed — a function, not a person.

Verdict: give Cairns exactly this one line in the cold open. Everything else there stays behavioral.
